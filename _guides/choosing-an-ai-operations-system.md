There is a lot being sold in this category, and much of it is the same fifty prompts with a
different cover. Three tests separate the useful from the repackaged, and you can apply all
three before paying.

## Test 1: does a prompt contain anything a general AI would not produce unprompted?

This is the sharpest test available.

Open the sample. Find a prompt for drafting an estimate. If it says something close to *"write
a professional estimate for [job]"*, you are buying a sentence you could have written. A free
assistant will produce the same output.

A prompt that earns its price carries constraints the model would not have applied on its own:

- a required exclusions block
- an instruction never to state a price
- a routing rule sending permit questions to a human
- a requirement to mark unrecorded information rather than infer it
- an explicit note about what to do with a safety concern

Those are the difference between being told a tool exists and being handed the configured
instrument.

## Test 2: does it say what AI must never do?

A system built by someone who understands licensed trades will state the boundaries plainly,
usually in the first few pages. Pricing, code, permits, safety.

If a package is silent on this, that silence is informative. It usually means the author has
not thought about what happens when the output is wrong, which means you will be the one
finding out.

Be especially wary of anything that presents AI as capable of diagnosing a fault, estimating
a repair cost, or advising a customer directly. Those are the three claims that most reliably
indicate the author has never carried the liability.

## Test 3: is there a review step?

Polished text reads as verified text. Any system that assumes its output can be sent
unreviewed is unsafe in a business where the output becomes a representation.

Look for a checklist, a quality gate, a sign-off procedure — something that sits between the
draft and the customer. Its presence tells you the author expected the model to be wrong
sometimes, which is the correct expectation.

## What to weigh less than you might expect

**File count.** Ten substantive documents beat forty thin ones, and volume is the easiest
thing to manufacture.

**Claimed time savings.** Any package quoting a specific figure — *save ten hours a week* — is
quoting a number it cannot support, because it has no data about your business. That is a
claim discipline signal, and it applies to the whole package.

**Case studies and testimonials.** Easy to fabricate, hard to verify, and their absence in a
new product is honest rather than suspicious.

**Vendor lock-in.** A system tied to one specific AI provider is a system that breaks when you
switch. The good ones name no vendor.

## What to weigh more

**Customization support.** Every template needs your warranty terms, exclusions, payment terms
and service area. A package that makes you retrofit each document individually will not get
adopted. One that captures your business facts once, and has every template inherit them, will.

**A sequencing plan.** The reason most operational purchases fail is that they are bought in
one evening and adopted never. A package with a week-by-week plan is taking adoption
seriously.

**A standing review rhythm.** Adoption without a recurring check decays in about three weeks —
not because anyone objects, but because nothing brings it back to attention.

## Questions to ask before paying

1. Can I see a full sample prompt, not a description of one?
2. Which decisions does this explicitly prohibit AI from making?
3. What do I have to do myself before this is usable?
4. Does it require a specific AI vendor?
5. What is the licence — one business, or can my franchisees use it?
6. Is there a refund path if it is not a fit?

An honest seller answers all six without hedging. The answers to 2 and 3 tell you the most.

## A closing note on expectations

An AI operations system does not fix an unclear process — documenting a bad procedure produces
a written bad procedure. It does not fix a staffing shortage; it reduces drafting, not field
work. And it does not improve technical quality, because nothing in it touches the work in the
field.

What it can do is take back the drafting hours and make delegation possible, because things
that were only in your head are finally written down. That is worth paying for. It is also
worth being precise about, which is why it is stated plainly here rather than dressed up.
