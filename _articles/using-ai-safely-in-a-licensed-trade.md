A licensed contractor carries accountability that does not transfer. If a wrong answer goes
out under your business name, "the software said so" is not an explanation. That constraint
shapes every sensible use of AI in the trades, and it is the reason generic advice about
AI productivity does not survive contact with a plumbing company.

The good news is that the constraint is workable, and the line falls in a fairly obvious
place once you look at it directly.

## Where the value actually is

The work AI absorbs well is written and repetitive, and there is more of it in a small trade
business than most owners consciously account for.

| Task | Why AI suits it |
|---|---|
| Field notes into job records | Restructuring fragments into a consistent form |
| Scope and exclusions drafting | Applying the same structure to every job |
| Customer messages | One voice regardless of who is typing |
| Procedure documentation | Extracting what is in your head by questioning |
| Message triage | Sorting a backlog by stated urgency |
| Objection responses | Preparing consistent answers in advance |

What these share: a person supplies the facts, the model supplies the structure, and a person
checks the result. None of them asks the model to know something only a licensed person knows.

## The four hard boundaries

### Pricing

AI does not set, estimate or imply a price. Your pricing depends on your costs, your market
and your margin. A model has none of that and will still produce a confident number if asked,
because producing plausible text is what it does. An invented price in a customer-facing
document is a representation you may be held to.

### Code compliance

AI does not state whether work is or is not code-compliant. Codes vary by jurisdiction, are
amended locally, and are interpreted by inspectors who do not read your chat history. The
correct output is a routing instruction: *permit and code requirements to be confirmed by the
company prior to scheduling*.

### Permit requirements

Same reasoning, same routing. Concealed conditions are the most common trigger for a permit
scope change, which is exactly the moment nobody wants a guess.

### Safety determinations

AI does not decide whether a condition is safe, and never tells a customer to operate, shut
off, reset or bypass anything. This one is not negotiable in either direction: it protects the
customer, and it protects you.

## What must never be entered

Independent of the boundaries above, some information should not go into a public AI tool at
all:

- Payment card numbers, bank details, any financial account information.
- Government identification numbers, dates of birth, medical information.
- Employee records, disciplinary records, pay information.
- Confidential business information or anything under a non-disclosure agreement.
- Photographs showing a customer's home interior, documents or belongings.
- Passwords, access codes, alarm codes, key box combinations.

Where a customer's name or address is genuinely needed, use the minimum. Most drafting tasks
work perfectly well with the customer described as "the customer".

Also worth checking once, before your first real job note goes in: most tools have a setting
controlling whether your conversations are used to improve their models. For business use,
turn it off.

## The review step

The most common failure is not bad output. It is staff pasting output without reading it,
because it *looks* finished.

A one-minute review before anything is sent catches nearly all of it:

- No price appears unless an authorised person put it there.
- No statement that anything is code-compliant, permitted, safe or unsafe.
- No instruction telling a customer to operate or shut off anything.
- No promise of an outcome, lifespan, or that a problem will not recur.
- Nothing appears that was not in the source material.
- Warranty terms quoted match the written warranty word for word.

That last one deserves emphasis. Warranty language is the highest-risk sentence you send, and
paraphrase has a way of becoming more generous than intended.

## Putting it in writing

A written policy is worth more than an informal habit, for two reasons. It gives staff a
standard to point at instead of guessing, and it gives you something to review when something
goes wrong.

It does not need to be long. What it needs to contain: what AI may be used for, the four
prohibited decisions, the information that must never be entered, the mandatory review step,
and the conditions under which someone stops and escalates rather than proceeding.

Then have people read and sign it — not as bureaucracy, but because the act of signing is
what makes staff read it once properly.

## Where this leaves you

AI in a licensed trade is neither a transformation nor a threat. It is a drafting tool that
removes a specific, well-defined category of work: the writing you currently do after dinner.

The businesses that get value from it are the ones that decided in advance what it is not
allowed to do, and then held that line when it would have been convenient not to.
