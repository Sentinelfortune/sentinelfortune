A technician writing on a phone in a crawlspace produces fragments. The office needs a record
that supports an invoice and still makes sense when the customer calls back in March.

## The seven fields

1. **Presenting complaint** — what the customer actually reported, in their words.
2. **Equipment** — make, model, serial, approximate age, location on site.
3. **Findings** — what was observed or measured. Numbers where numbers exist.
4. **Work performed** — actions taken, in order.
5. **Parts used** — description and quantity.
6. **System state on departure** — operational, partially operational, shut down, or isolated.
7. **Recommended follow-up** — deferred or declined work, and that the customer was told.

## The two that get skipped

Six and seven. They are also the two that matter most later.

**System state on departure** is what the next person needs before they arrive. If a service
was left isolated and nobody wrote it down, the next visit starts blind.

**Recommended follow-up** is your record that the customer was informed. Without it, a
deferred recommendation becomes a dispute about whether you mentioned it.

## On "tested ok"

It is not a finding. It is a phrase that means the technician was finished. If a measurement
was taken, record the measurement. If one was not, "no measurement taken" is more honest and
more useful than an implied all-clear.

## Why measurements matter more than adjectives

"Low on refrigerant" is an opinion. "Measured 42 uF on a 45 uF rated capacitor" is a fact that
still means something in six months, to a different technician, in front of a customer who is
questioning the invoice.

The difference costs nothing at the time and decides whether the record is usable later. Where
a measurement was taken, record the number and the unit. Where a specification exists, record
it as written on the plate rather than from memory.

## The isolation record

For any job touching gas, water, refrigerant or electrical work, one more thing belongs in the
note: what was isolated, at which point, by what method, and whether it was restored before
departure.

If a service was left isolated, the note must say so and say who was told. This is the single
piece of information whose absence creates real risk rather than merely administrative
friction — and it is routinely omitted because the technician knows, and assumes the record is
for billing.

It is not. It is for the next person.

## Making it stick

Do not solve this by asking for longer notes. Solve it by making the form ask for the seven
fields directly, then auditing weekly to find which field goes missing most often.

If the same field is missing across most notes, that is a template problem rather than a person
problem, and changing the form fixes the week. Treat the audit as a coaching input, never as a
performance rating — note quality reflects where the note gets written, not how much anyone
cares.
