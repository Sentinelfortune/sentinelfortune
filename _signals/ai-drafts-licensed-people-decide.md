Most owners in the trades have already tried an AI assistant. They asked it to write a
customer email or an estimate, got back something that read plausibly but was missing every
exclusion that matters, invented a price, or stated something about code that no licensed
contractor could safely send. They tried twice and stopped.

That reaction is correct as far as it goes. A general-purpose AI given a blank instruction
produces generic output, because a blank instruction is generic.

## The division that makes it work

**Drafting** is the part of your written work that is slow, repetitive and mostly mechanical:
turning a technician's fragments into a job record, structuring a scope of work, writing the
fourth follow-up message this week.

**Deciding** is the part that requires a licence, judgement, or accountability: what something
costs, whether it meets code, whether a permit is needed, whether a condition is safe.

AI is genuinely good at the first. It must never be allowed near the second.

## What happens when the line moves

The failure is rarely dramatic. It looks like an estimate that went out with a price the
model suggested and nobody checked. Or a post-job summary that told a customer their system
was "safe and up to code" because that phrasing sounded reassuring.

Both are representations your business now owns.

## The practical version

Write the boundary down, give it to whoever uses the tools, and put a review step between
the draft and the customer. Not because your staff are careless — because polished text reads
as verified text, and it is not.
